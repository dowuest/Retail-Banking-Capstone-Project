# U.S. Consumer Lending: Market Economics and Mega-Bank Positioning in 2024

**Credit cards remain the undisputed profit engine of consumer banking, generating a 3.87% ROA — roughly 3.5× the industry average** — and the top six mega-banks collectively hold over $630 billion in card receivables. Total U.S. household debt reached a record **$18.04 trillion** at year-end 2024, with consumer lending revenue pools exceeding **$500 billion** across all products. The critical strategic finding: mega-banks are structurally advantaged in only two consumer products (cards and HELOCs), have ceded mortgage origination to non-banks, face fragmented competition in auto, and have largely exited personal lending. The 2020–2024 period produced the most volatile swing in consumer lending economics in a generation — from pandemic-era mortgage windfalls and record-low charge-offs to a sharp normalization that compressed origination margins and pushed card losses to 13-year highs before stabilization in late 2024.

---

## The $18 trillion consumer balance sheet

U.S. household debt outstanding reached **$18.04 trillion** in Q4 2024, up $530 billion (+3.0%) year-over-year, per the NY Fed Household Debt and Credit Report. Mortgage debt dominates at **$12.61 trillion** (70% of the total), followed by auto loans at **$1.66 trillion**, student loans at **$1.62 trillion**, credit cards at **$1.21 trillion**, and HELOCs at **$396 billion**. Personal loans, tracked separately by TransUnion, totaled approximately **$249 billion**.

The Federal Reserve's G.19 release — which excludes real estate-secured debt — shows total consumer credit at **$5,145.5 billion** as of December 2024, comprising $1,382.2 billion in revolving credit and $3,763.4 billion in nonrevolving credit. Growth decelerated to **+2.4%** in 2024 from +7.6% in 2022 and +2.6% in 2023, reflecting consumer caution and tighter bank underwriting standards.

## Revenue pools exceed $500 billion, dominated by cards

Total U.S. consumer lending generates an estimated **$530–580 billion** in annual revenue across all products and lender types. Credit cards alone account for roughly 60% of the total revenue pool despite representing just 7% of outstanding balances — a testament to the product's extraordinary yield economics.

In 2024, origination economics were razor-thin: the MBA reported average production profit of just **$443 per loan** (10 basis points), the first positive full year after consecutive losses of $301/loan in 2022 and $1,056/loan in 2023.

## Credit cards generate 3–4× the ROA of any other consumer product

The Federal Reserve's 2024 Report to Congress on credit card bank profitability confirms that credit card operations delivered a pretax **ROA of 3.87%**, compared with **1.12%** for the FDIC-insured banking industry overall. This makes cards **the single most profitable product in consumer banking** by a wide margin.

**Profitability by product — credit card banks vs. all banks (2024, % of assets):**

|--------|-------------------|----------------------|

| Noninterest income | **6.34%** | 1.30% |
| Noninterest expense | 9.03% | 2.45% |
| Provision for losses | 3.62% | 0.38% |
| **Pretax ROA** | **3.87%** | **1.38%** |

Historical credit card ROA has averaged **4–5%** since 2001, peaking at 7.65% in 2006 and 6.93% in 2021 (when charge-offs hit historic lows of 1.63%). The only negative year was 2009 (-5.33% during the financial crisis). In 2024, ROA recovered from a 2023 trough of 3.31% despite charge-offs reaching 4.58% — interest income growth and moderating provisions drove the improvement.

## Product-by-product economics reveal starkly different value propositions

### Credit cards: the $310B+ revenue machine

Credit cards are the crown jewel of consumer banking for three reinforcing reasons: **extraordinarily high yields** (average APR of 22.8% on interest-bearing accounts, producing NIM of ~10% after funding costs), **dual revenue streams** (interest income from the ~50% of cardholders who revolve plus interchange income from all transactions), and **immediate rate repricing** (variable-rate product that passes through Fed rate changes within one billing cycle).

The top 10 issuers hold **83% of total outstanding balances**. JPMorgan Chase leads with 17.27% share by receivables, followed by American Express, Citigroup, Capital One, and Bank of America. The Capital One–Discover merger (announced February 2024, approximately **$35 billion** all-stock deal) would create the largest issuer by outstanding balances and give Capital One its own payment network — a structural advantage that would exempt it from proposed interchange routing mandates.

Card charge-offs peaked at **4.69%** in Q3 2024 (the highest level since 2011) before moderating to **4.58%** in Q4, as post-pandemic vintage seasoning played through. JPMorgan's card NCO rate of 3.30% meaningfully outperformed the industry, reflecting its prime-heavy portfolio mix. Capital One's domestic card NCO rate of approximately 6.15% reflects its deliberate subprime/near-prime strategy — higher losses offset by substantially higher yields (7.11% consolidated NIM).

**Key risk:** Interchange revenue faces structural headwinds. The April 2024 Visa/Mastercard antitrust settlement would reduce interchange by **10 basis points** over five years and introduce a **1.25% cap** on standard (non-premium) cards. The Credit Card Competition Act of 2026 — which has bipartisan support and a presidential endorsement — could reduce interchange revenue by up to 50% for covered issuers if enacted. This would not eliminate card profitability but would compress the ~$148.5 billion interchange pool meaningfully.

### Mortgages: origination is barely viable, servicing is the value

Total mortgage originations reached approximately **$1.79 trillion** in 2024, a modest recovery from the $1.50 trillion trough in 2023 but still a fraction of the **$4.3–4.5 trillion** produced in 2020–2021. The refinance channel remains effectively closed — refi share was just 16–21% of originations in 2024 versus 60–65% in 2020–2021 — as the average outstanding mortgage rate of approximately 3.5–4.0% sits well below the 2024 origination rate of **6.72%**.

The industry's gain-on-sale margin compressed to approximately **118 basis points** in 2024 (per UWM's Q3 disclosure), down from 400–450 basis points during the 2020 boom. The MBA's cost-to-originate study showed total production expenses of **$11,076 per loan** against revenues of $11,520 per loan — yielding a razor-thin **$443 per loan** ($10 basis points) average profit. Only 56% of firms were profitable on production alone; servicing income lifted the figure to 68%.

**Non-banks now dominate origination.** The NCRC's 2024 HMDA analysis shows non-bank share at **53.3%** of all mortgage-related originations, with banks at 30.1% and credit unions at 16.6%. United Wholesale Mortgage led all originators with **$137.8 billion** in 2024 volume, followed by Rocket Mortgage at $92.2 billion. Among banks, only Chase ($37.5 billion), Bank of America ($29.5 billion), and U.S. Bank ($26.0 billion) ranked in the top 10. The Kansas City Fed documents the key dynamic: banks gain origination share when rates rise (cheaper deposit funding) but lose share during easing cycles when non-banks' operational speed and broker channel access dominate.

Mortgage servicing has become the primary value driver. Mr. Cooper, through aggressive MSR acquisitions (including the Flagstar portfolio), became the **#1 servicer** in 2024 with over $1.1 trillion in unpaid principal balance, surpassing JPMorgan. Among the top 10 servicers, non-banks now hold 55% of servicing volume by UPB. Rocket Mortgage's acquisition of Mr. Cooper and Redfin signals the creation of an integrated origination-servicing-real estate ecosystem that traditional banks will struggle to replicate.

### Auto: fragmented and competitively challenged for banks

Auto loans outstanding reached **$1.66 trillion** with approximately **$704 billion** in originations during 2024. The market is unusually fragmented: captive finance companies (Toyota Motor Credit, GM Financial, Ford Motor Credit) hold **31.4%** share, banks 25.0%, credit unions 20.1%, and other lenders 23.5%. Captives dominate new-vehicle financing at **61.8%** share — largely through OEM-subsidized rates (0.0–1.9% APR promotions) that banks cannot match.

Average auto loan rates rose sharply: new-vehicle APRs reached **6.35%** and used-vehicle APRs **11.62%** in Q4 2024, up from roughly 4% and 8% respectively in 2020. Auto delinquency rates are elevated, with 90+ day serious delinquency reaching approximately **4.6%** at year-end 2024 per the NY Fed. Subprime auto borrowers (30+ DPD) reached **15.78%** delinquency in Q3 2024, the highest since tracking began in 2000. Wells Fargo's auto charge-off rate of **0.77%** (Q4 2024) was below industry average, reflecting its prime-focused book.

Banks are **not structurally advantaged** in auto lending. Captives win on rate (manufacturer subsidies), credit unions win on pricing (lower deposit-funded cost of funds, typically 50–100 basis points below banks), and fintech-enabled platforms are improving the consumer experience. Banks' primary advantages are cross-sell potential and balance sheet scale for prime portfolios.

### HELOC: the highest-conviction growth opportunity for banks

HELOC balances outstanding reached **$396 billion** in Q4 2024, growing **10%+ year-over-year** — the 11th consecutive quarterly increase after a decade of post-crisis decline. Originations totaled approximately **$201 billion** in 2024 (ATTOM/HELN estimate). The growth thesis is compelling: total tappable home equity approaches **$12 trillion** (ICE Mortgage Monitor), with average tappable equity per borrower of approximately $299,000. The mortgage rate lock-in effect (borrowers with sub-4% first mortgages refusing to refinance) makes HELOCs the rational way to access equity.

HELOC credit quality is exceptional. The average FICO score for new HELOC borrowers was **771** in 2024 (up from 742 in 2023), average combined loan-to-value at closing was **62%**, and utilization rates ran at approximately **37–42%** of credit lines. Charge-off rates on HELOCs are near zero — residential real estate charge-offs at commercial banks run 0.01–0.05%. This product offers estimated ROA of **1.0–2.0%** with minimal credit risk.

Banks are **structurally advantaged** in HELOC lending because of their first-lien position knowledge, deposit cross-sell relationships, branch networks, and regulatory familiarity. Non-bank entrants are emerging (17 HELOC securitizations in 2024 for $5.4 billion in private-label deals), but banks retain the dominant position. MBA research confirms lenders expect HELOC outstanding to grow approximately **9.5–10%** annually through 2026.

### Personal loans: banks have ceded the market to fintechs

Personal loan balances totaled approximately **$249 billion** in 2024, with an estimated 20.8 million originations. The defining trend is fintech dominance: from less than 1% share in 2010, fintech lenders now service **more unsecured personal loan debt than banks and credit unions combined** (TransUnion, 2025). SoFi alone originated a record **$5.3 billion** in personal loans in Q4 2024 (+63% YoY).

Most mega-banks have exited or scaled back personal lending. Goldman Sachs' Marcus division — the highest-profile bank attempt to enter — **lost over $3 billion** since December 2020 before halting new originations in 2023 and selling its portfolio at a discount. Bank of America and JPMorgan Chase do not offer personal loans. Wells Fargo offers them only to existing customers with 1+ year account history. The rationale for bank withdrawal is clear: CECL accounting punishes growth in unsecured portfolios, losses run 3–5% (versus near-zero for mortgages), and fintechs have 30–50% lower origination costs through digital-first platforms.

### BNPL: growing but still marginal at ~1% of card volume

Buy Now, Pay Later generated approximately **$109 billion** in U.S. gross merchandise volume in 2024, up 14.8% year-over-year. Klarna led globally ($105 billion GMV), followed by PayPal Pay Later ($33 billion global BNPL volume), and Affirm ($31 billion). Klarna achieved its first full-year profit in 2024 (**$21 million** net income), driven largely by AI-powered cost reduction (an AI customer service agent handled 2.3 million conversations, equivalent to 700 full-time employees).

BNPL's charge-off rate declined to **1.83%** in 2023 (CFPB six-firm data) with average loss rates as a percentage of GMV just 0.92%. The short loan duration (~6 weeks for pay-in-four products) enables approximately 9× capital turns per year, improving unit economics despite thin per-transaction margins.

For banks, BNPL represents a **complementary threat rather than an existential one** — it captures roughly 1% of total credit card spending volume. Banks are responding with card-linked installment features: Chase My Plan, Citi Flex Pay, and Amex Plan It. Apple discontinued its proprietary Pay Later service in June 2024, instead enabling bank-issued installments within Apple Pay, effectively ceding the BNPL infrastructure to card issuers.

## How market economics transformed from 2020 to 2024

The 2020–2024 period produced extraordinary volatility in consumer lending economics, driven by pandemic stimulus, the fastest rate-hiking cycle in four decades, and subsequent credit normalization.

**Charge-off rates by product (Q4 each year, all commercial banks, seasonally adjusted):**

| Product | 2020 | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 |
|---------|------|------|------|------|------|
| Credit Cards | 2.68% | 1.63% | 2.51% | 4.19% | **4.58%** |
| All Consumer | 1.50% | 0.91% | 1.63% | 2.64% | **2.98%** |
| Other Consumer | 0.55% | 0.36% | 0.87% | 1.15% | **1.19%** |
| Total Loans | 0.39% | 0.19% | 0.33% | 0.60% | **0.65%** |

*Source: Federal Reserve Board, Charge-Off and Delinquency Rates on Loans and Leases at Commercial Banks.*

**FDIC industry metrics, 2020–2024:**

| Metric | 2020 | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 |
|--------|------|------|------|------|------|
| Industry ROA | 0.72% | 1.23% | 1.11% | 1.09% | **1.12%** |
| Net Interest Margin | 2.82% | 2.54% | 2.95% | 3.30% | **3.22%** |

| Credit Card NCO (FDIC) | — | — | — | — | **4.57%** |

**Phase 1 (2020–2021): Pandemic windfall.** Mortgage originations surged to $4.3–4.5 trillion annually on sub-3% rates, producing record gain-on-sale margins of 400+ basis points. Credit card balances *declined* 17% as stimulus payments fueled mass deleveraging. Card charge-offs fell to an all-time low of 1.63% by Q4 2021, pushing card bank ROA to **6.93%**. Consumer lending was extraordinarily profitable across every dimension.

**Phase 2 (2022–2023): Rate shock.** The Fed raised rates 525 basis points. Mortgage originations collapsed by 60%, gain-on-sale margins compressed below 200 basis points, and the average independent mortgage bank **lost $1,056 per loan** in 2023. Card balances surged as consumers resumed revolving — balances grew from $770 billion (Q1 2021 trough) past $1 trillion by late 2023. Charge-offs normalized rapidly: card NCOs rose from 1.63% to 4.19% in just eight quarters. Bank deposit costs increased sharply, compressing NIMs from the 2023 peak of 3.30%.

**Phase 3 (2024): Stabilization.** Card charge-offs peaked at 4.69% in Q3 2024 and edged down to 4.58% in Q4 — still **109 basis points above pre-pandemic averages** per the FDIC but no longer accelerating. Card bank ROA recovered to **3.87%** as interest income growth (fueled by APR spread expansion) outpaced credit losses. The critical spread dynamic: credit card APR spreads over the federal funds rate expanded from 8.2% in 2007 to **16.8%** by end-2024, meaning issuers retained margin even as the Fed began cutting rates. Mortgage origination turned barely positive ($443/loan). Industry NIM stabilized at 3.22%, slightly below the pre-pandemic average of 3.25%. Total FDIC-insured bank net income reached a record **$268.2 billion**.

## Competitive landscape: where banks win and where they don't

The competitive dynamics differ radically by product, and mega-banks must be clear-eyed about where they hold structural advantages versus where they face permanent disadvantage.

**Credit cards — mega-bank fortress.** The top 10 issuers control **83% of outstanding balances**, and the top 5 capture **69.1% of purchase volume**. Scale advantages in data, underwriting, marketing, fraud prevention, and network economics create a formidable moat. Fintechs and neobanks have not meaningfully penetrated credit card issuance, which requires banking licenses, substantial capital, and sophisticated credit risk management. The primary competitive threat is from specialists: Capital One (after Discover merger) will control its own network, and American Express commands the premium segment with $1,168 billion in purchase volume. Interchange regulation is the structural risk, not new entrants.

**Mortgages — non-banks winning origination, banks retreating.** Bank share of originations fell **below one-third** in 2024. United Wholesale Mortgage and Rocket Mortgage together originated over $230 billion — more than all six mega-banks combined. Non-banks dominate through speed, digital experience, broker channel access, and specialization in government lending (FHA/VA). Banks retain advantages in portfolio lending (jumbo and non-QM products), mortgage servicing scale, and cross-sell economics. Non-bank servicing growth has drawn FSOC attention as a systemic risk; non-banks held **55%** of top-50 servicer volume by UPB at year-end 2023.

**Auto — fragmented, no structural bank advantage.** Banks hold just **25%** market share and are sandwiched between captive finance companies (31.4% share, dominating new vehicles at 62%) and credit unions (20.1% share, competing aggressively on rate). Banks' delinquency rates of approximately **1.6%** (60+ DPD) are well below monoline lenders (16.6%) but comparable to captives and credit unions (~0.8%). Wells Fargo's decision to ramp originations 52% in Q4 2024 suggests selective reengagement, but the product does not generate outsized returns.

**Personal loans — fintechs dominant, banks largely absent.** Fintech lenders now originate more than half of all personal loan volume. SoFi, LendingClub (now a bank charter), Upstart, and Prosper have built data-driven origination engines that achieve 30–50% lower acquisition costs than traditional banks. Bank 60+ day delinquency rates of **1.2%** are far better than fintech rates of 3.8%, but most mega-banks concluded the product is capital-inefficient relative to cards and not worth the operational investment.

**HELOC — clear bank structural advantage.** Banks dominate HELOC origination through first-lien knowledge, existing customer relationships, and branch-based advisory. Average FICO of 771 delivers pristine credit quality. The $12 trillion tappable equity base, combined with the mortgage rate lock-in effect, creates a secular growth runway. This is the product where mega-banks have the clearest competitive moat and the most attractive risk-adjusted returns outside of cards.

## Strategic implications for traditional retail banks

**Protect and invest in cards above all else.** Credit cards generate 3–4× the ROA of any other consumer product and are insulated from the competitive pressures affecting mortgages, auto, and personal lending. The critical strategic imperative is to prepare for interchange compression: if the Credit Card Competition Act passes or the Visa/Mastercard settlement reduces interchange by 10+ basis points, banks must be ready to restructure rewards programs and shift revenue mix further toward interest income and annual fees. JPMorgan's **32% ROE** in its consumer segment demonstrates what is possible with scale and execution in cards.

**Aggressively pursue HELOC as the primary growth vehicle in secured lending.** With $12 trillion in tappable equity and borrowers locked into sub-4% first mortgages, HELOC is a generational opportunity. Banks should invest in digitizing HELOC origination (current pull-through rates are just 46%), expanding credit lines for existing customers, and building data-driven cross-sell engines from their mortgage servicing portfolios. Expected annual growth of 9–10% in HELOC outstanding through 2026 represents the fastest-growing segment in consumer lending.

**Maintain mortgage for relationship value, not origination volume.** Competing with UWM and Rocket on origination volume is a losing strategy. Instead, banks should focus on portfolio lending for high-value clients (jumbo, physician, and specialty products), leveraging servicing portfolios for cross-sell, and maintaining competence in home lending as a customer acquisition and retention tool. The origination P&L will remain volatile and marginal.

**Manage auto selectively and watch credit quality closely.** Auto lending generates moderate returns with no structural bank advantage. Subprime auto delinquencies at 15.78% (30+ DPD) signal stress in lower credit tiers. Banks should maintain prime auto portfolios for cross-sell and relationship value but avoid stretching into near-prime or subprime segments where captives and specialty lenders have better economics.

**Monitor AI-driven disintermediation of the deposit franchise.** McKinsey's 2025 Global Banking Annual Review warns that if just 5–10% of checking account balances migrate to higher-yield alternatives through AI-powered consumer agents, deposit profits could decline by **20%+**. Since cheap deposits are the foundational funding advantage that makes consumer lending profitable for banks, protecting the deposit franchise is existential. Banks that delay AI adoption could see ROTE decline by 1–2 percentage points, while AI pioneers could gain up to 4 points of advantage.

## Conclusion

The U.S. consumer lending market in 2024 is a tale of radical product-level differentiation. Credit cards stand alone as the profit engine — generating **$310–330 billion** in annual revenue at nearly **4% ROA** — and mega-banks are well-positioned to retain dominance given the 83% concentration among top issuers. HELOCs represent the clearest growth opportunity, combining bank structural advantages with a $12 trillion addressable market and pristine credit quality. Mortgages have become a relationship product, not a profit center, with origination barely viable and servicing shifting to non-banks. Auto remains commoditized, personal lending has been ceded to fintechs, and BNPL is complementary rather than threatening. The overarching strategic challenge is not which products to enter but which economics to protect: the card-deposit nexus that generates outsized returns depends on interchange durability and deposit franchise stability — both of which face regulatory and technological headwinds that will define the next strategic cycle.
