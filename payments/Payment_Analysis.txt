

=== SEITE 01 ===

US Payments & Transactions Market: Strategic Analysis

Senior Strategy Consultant Perspective | March 2026

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The US payments market is undergoing the most structurally significant transformation ina
generation. Three forces are converging simultaneously: (1) real-time rail proliferation
(FedNow, RTP) threatening card economics at the margin; (2) stablecoin legitimization via the
GENIUS Act (July 2025), opening a new settlement layer that could bypass correspondent
banking entirely; and (3) Al-driven intelligence shifting competitive advantage from
infrastructure ownership toward data density and model quality. The result is a market where
the traditional integrated stack — bank + network + processor + issuer — is fragmenting
horizontally, with software and intelligence layers capturing an increasing share of value. Yet
cards remain structurally dominant in consumer payments, and incumbents with scale, data, and
distribution are not standing still. The next 3-5 years will determine which players become
utilities and which become intelligence control points — and the strategic choices made now will
define the industry structure for the following decade.

1. MARKET STRUCTURE AND VALUE POOLS

The End-to-End US Payments Value Chain

The US payments system is best understood as a set of overlapping value chains, not a single
stack. Each payment type has its own rails, economics, and players — but AI, APIs, and
regulatory shifts are forcing convergence.

Layer 1 — Issuers: Banks and credit unions originating cards and accounts. Revenue:
interchange, interest income, annual fees, FX spreads on international cards. Key players:
JPMorgan Chase, Citibank, Bank of America, Capital One, American Express (issuer/network
hybrid), Synchrony Financial, Bread Financial. Issuer economics are heavily reliant on
interchange ($100B+ annually in US credit and debit interchange revenue) and revolving credit
spread.

Layer 2 — Card Networks: Visa, Mastercard (four-party model); Amex, Discover (three-party
model). Revenue: network/assessment fees (~0.11-0.13% per transaction), data services, value-
added services (fraud tools, analytics, tokenization). In fiscal year 2025, Visa processed $14
trillion in total payments volume across 258 billion transactions, generating $40 billion in net
revenue. Mastercard handled $9.2 trillion in GDV. Their moats are ubiquity, token vaults, fraud

infrastructure, and rule-setting authority — not transaction processing per se. These are the
strongest structural positions in global payments.

Layer 3 — Acquirers and Processors: Merchant-facing. Revenue: acquiring spread, gateway fees,
value-added services. Collectively, merchant acquirers handle more than $9 trillion in annual
transaction volume in the US. Key players: FIS (Worldpay), Fiserv (Clover), Global Payments,
JPMorgan Merchant Services, Stripe, Adyen, Block/Square. Commoditization pressure is acute
here, especially in gateway and plain-vanilla processing.

Layer 4 — Gateways and Orchestration: Connecting merchants to acquirers, routing
transactions. Standalone gateway revenue is compressing toward zero as orchestration layers
absorb the function. Players: Spreedly, Primer, Gr4vy, Checkout.com, Stripe (vertically
integrated). Orchestration is the fastest-growing layer — merchants want multi-acquirer routing,
least-cost routing, and authorization rate optimization simultaneously.

Layer 5 — Core Banking and Treasury Infrastructure: BaaS platforms (Galileo/SoFi, Margeta,
Unit, Synctera), core banking (FIS, Fiserv, Jack Henry), treasury infrastructure (Kyriba, Modern
Treasury, TreasuryPrime). These are the rails under the fintechs. Revenue: SaaS fees, per-
transaction fees, float on held balances.

Layer 6 — Correspondent Banks and Wholesale Infrastructure: SWIFT messaging, FedWire,
CHIPS for large-value settlement, correspondent networks for cross-border. Revenue: wire fees,
FX spreads, float, correspondent account balances. Under structural pressure from RTP, FedNow,
and stablecoins.

Layer 7 — Wallets and Super-Apps: Apple Pay, Google Pay, PayPal/Venmo, Cash App, WeChat
Pay (limited US). Revenue models vary: Apple earns ~15bps per Apple Pay transaction on credit,
PayPal earns transaction take-rates of ~1.5-2%. Wallets are becoming the consumer identity
layer, not just a payment credential holder.

Layer 8 — Crypto and Stablecoin Infrastructure: Circle (USDC), Paxos (PYUSD), Coinbase,
Fireblocks (custody/settlement rails), Ripple (ODL for cross-border). Revenue: issuance float on
stablecoin reserves (T-bill yield on ~$50-100B+ USD-pegged reserves), custody fees, exchange
spreads, payment network fees. The volume of outstanding USD stablecoins grew to nearly $280
billion at year-end 2025 from about $25 billion in 2020.

Layer 9 — Fraud, Risk, and Compliance Infrastructure: Featurespace, Sardine, Sift, Socure,
Alloy, Persona, Onfido, Comply Advantage, unit21, Hawk AI. Revenue: SaaS, per-API-call, per-
transaction risk scoring. This layer is growing faster than any other as AI shifts it from rules-
based to model-driven.

2. PAYMENT TYPES: STRATEGIC ANALYSIS

Cards (Credit, Debit, Prepaid — Consumer and Commercial)


=== SEITE 04 ===

Current role: Still the dominant non-cash payment method. Credit cards dominated in-store
transactions at 70% in 2024. US consumers hold ~170 million credit cards; credit card debt
exceeded $900B in 2023. Cards are the default payment UX for e-commerce, in-store, and
recurring billing.

Economics: Consumer credit: interchange 1.5-2.4% + flat fee. Debit (unregulated): ~1.5%
interchange. Debit (regulated, Durbin): capped at ~0.21% + $0.05. Commercial/corporate cards:
2.0-2.8%, higher interchange due to card-not-present and B2B premiums. Merchant discount
rate aggregate ~1.8-2.5% in most verticals, higher for premium and travel cards. A standard US
consumer credit card transaction might carry an interchange rate of 1.50% + $0.10, while a
premium rewards card could reach 2.40% + $0.10 or higher.

Key players: Visa, Mastercard (networks); JPMorgan Chase, Amex, Citi, Capital One, BofA (top
issuers); Fiserv, FIS, Adyen, Stripe (acquiring side). Margeta leads card issuing infrastructure for
fintechs.

Strengths: Global acceptance ubiquity. Consumer protection (chargebacks, zero liability).
Rewards and benefits economics that are self-reinforcing. Network effects on both merchant and
consumer sides. Tokenization infrastructure is now deeply embedded in mobile wallets (Apple
Pay = card credentials, not a new rail).

Weaknesses: High cost to merchants, especially SMBs. Interchange attracts regulatory scrutiny.
Credit Card Competition Act (CCCA) — if passed, would force routing choice on credit cards
(analogous to Durbin for debit), threatening the Visa/Mastercard duopoly on credit card routing.
Chargebacks/disputes are structurally expensive for issuers and merchants alike.

Strategic outlook (3-5 years): Cards will remain dominant in consumer payments by volume
and value, but face two structural pressures: (1) incremental margin compression on interchange
from regulation and competition; (2) selective displacement by A2A/RTP in specific merchant
categories (utilities, rent, B2B). The networks themselves are well-positioned: Visa and
Mastercard's response to A2A is tokenization of accounts (Visa A2A), not resistance.
Commercial card is the fastest-growing sub-segment (expense management, AP automation,
virtual cards). Prepaid and BaaS-linked debit will grow as embedded finance platforms issue
white-label debit products.

ACH (Automated Clearing House)

Current role: The backbone of B2B and recurring consumer payments. Payroll, direct deposit, bill
pay, B2B supplier payments. Not real-time — next-day and same-day ACH are available but not
instant.

Economics: Very low per-transaction cost. Same-Day ACH has a network fee of $0.052 per
transaction. Volume: ~31 billion transactions and ~$80 trillion in value annually (Nacha 2024).


=== SEITE 05 ===

Per-transaction revenue is minimal — value capture is in treasury, float, and volume-driven
SaaS.

Key players: Federal Reserve (FedACH), EPN (operated by The Clearing House), Nacha
(governance). Access via bank treasuries, fintechs using Modern Treasury, Dwolla, Plaid, or
direct bank APIs.

Strengths: Near-universal bank coverage. Very low cost. Deeply embedded in payroll, direct
deposit, bill pay, B2B AP workflows.

Weaknesses: Not real-time (even Same-Day ACH settles in batches). Not irrevocable —
returns/reversals create fraud risk and operational complexity. Not 24/7. Poorly suited to high-
urgency or consumer-demand use cases.

Strategic outlook: ACH will retain dominance in high-value, non-urgent B2B flows for the next
5+ years. Same-Day ACH will grow but will increasingly be complemented (not replaced) by
FedNow and RTP for time-sensitive use cases. The Key risk: as FedNow reaches critical mass,
merchants and platforms may shift recurring consumer billing to A2A instant rails for lower cost
and faster float benefit.

RTP / FedNow / Instant A2A Payments

Current role: The most strategically important infrastructure shift in US payments in decades.
RTP (The Clearing House, launched 2017, now covering ~90% of US demand deposit accounts)
and FedNow (launched July 2023) are competing 24/7/365 instant payment rails. FedNow
expanded from 35 launch banks to more than 900 institutions by August 2024, with community
banks representing 78% of participants.

Economics: Network fees very low — $0.045 per transaction on FedNow. Revenue captures is in
treasury management, float benefit to receivers (immediate funds availability), reduced
chargeback exposure, and the ability to build new products on top (e.g., request-for-payment,
insurance claims, gig worker payouts, earned wage access).

Key players: Federal Reserve (FedNow), The Clearing House (RTP), bank participants, fintech
overlays (Dwolla, Modern Treasury, Plaid), and software orchestrators building on top.

Strengths: Real-time, 24/7, irrevocable (no chargebacks). Removes float drag. Enables new
payment flows that are impossible on ACH or card rails. Lower cost than cards for merchants
willing to accept the UX change.

Weaknesses: Irrevocability is a double-edged sword — no consumer protection equivalent to
card chargebacks. Authorized push payment (APP) fraud is a growing problem (significant in UK
after Faster Payments launch). Merchant acceptance requires new checkout UX; consumer
adoption is a chicken-and-egg problem. Credit functionality (BNPL, installments) must be
layered separately — no native credit.


=== SEITE 06 ===

Strategic outlook: The most important 3-5 year question in US payments. The consensus bullish
case: RTP/FedNow displace check and ACH in bill pay, insurance payouts, gig/contractor
payments, and B2B disbursements. The bearish case on card displacement: without consumer
protection parity and a rewards equivalent, consumers won't voluntarily shift from credit cards
for discretionary purchases. The honest answer: significant displacement in push-payment use
cases (payroll, earned wage access, insurance, government disbursements, B2B AP) but limited
consumer POS displacement through 2028 absent a regulatory forcing function or a merchant-
funded incentive structure. Non-obvious second-order effect: FedNow + open banking +
request-for-payment (RfP) protocols together could create a merchant-directed "pull A2A"
product competitive with debit cards for subscription billing — this is the real threat to regulated
debit interchange over the next 5 years.

Checks

Current role: In secular decline but still the largest payment instrument by dollar value in B2B.
Cash usage was approximately 20% of all transactions in 2021, and by 2024 the percentage
decreased to 16%. Checks follow a similar arc. Federal Reserve data shows checks declining ~7%
per year. B2B still accounts for ~60% of check volume; consumer check writing is approaching
residual.

Economics: Costly to process — bank revenue from check processing comes from service fees,
float during clearing, and overdraft fees triggered by bad check timing. Positive Pay, Check Fraud
Fraud services generate some incremental bank revenue. Total check clearing costs to the
industry are substantial but declining as volumes fall.

Key players: Federal Reserve (check processing services), FIS, Fiserv, bank back-offices. Fintech
challengers: Checkbook.io (digital check issuance), Paymentus (electronic bill pay replacing
check receipt).

Strategic outlook: Structural decline accelerating. The remaining check volume is sticky
because it is embedded in accounts payable/receivable software (QuickBooks, SAP, Oracle),
legal/real estate settlements, and segments that have not yet digitized. Digital check issuance
services (sending a PDF or printable check via email) are a transitional product, not a
destination. Check processing will become a utility service managed at declining scale by the
Fed and large banks. No strategic premium here — only cost management.

Domestic Wires (Fedwire, CHIPS)

Current role: High-value, same-day settlement for corporate treasury, real estate, securities
settlement, and interbank funding. CHIPS (Clearing House Interbank Payments System) settles
~$1.8T/day; Fedwire ~$4T/day. Not a consumer product.


=== SEITE 07 ===

Economics: Per-wire fee income for banks: $15-50 per outgoing wire (consumer), variable for
wholesale. CHIPS is net-settled with significant netting efficiency. Total wire fee revenue is
relatively modest (~$5-8B) compared to the dollar volume, because unit economics are
transaction fees on very large amounts.

Key players: Federal Reserve (Fedwire), The Clearing House (CHIPS), money-center banks
(JPMorgan, Citi, BofA, Wells Fargo). Corporate treasury management systems connect clients to
wire infrastructure.

Strategic outlook: Wires remain critical for large-value, time-sensitive institutional payments.
They are not being displaced by FedNow (different value threshold, different risk profile). The
real competitive threat: blockchain-based settlement (tokenized deposits, stablecoins) for
institutional cross-border flows, where JPMorgan's Onyx/JPM Coin has demonstrated that
banks themselves see the opportunity to disintermediate SWIFT-dependent correspondent
chains using internal ledgers.

SWIFT and Correspondent Banking

Current role: The messaging infrastructure (not a rail itself) connecting ~11,000 financial
institutions in 200+ countries. Used for international wire instructions. ISO 20022 migration is
now in full deployment phase.

Economics: High-margin for correspondent banks holding nostro/vostro accounts — float, FX
spread, wire fees, and crucially, the ability to cross-sell trade finance and treasury services to
respondent banks. Total correspondent banking revenue globally: ~$25-35B (wholesale). In the
US, money-center banks (JPMorgan, Citi, BofA, Wells) are the dominant USD correspondents.

Key players: SWIFT (messaging), JPMorgan (dominant USD correspondent), Citi (global
network), BofA, Wells Fargo. Challengers: Wise, Ripple (ODL), stablecoin rails.

Strengths: Deep integration into trade finance, documentary credit, securities settlement. ISO
20022 migration is adding rich data payloads that improve reconciliation and enable AI-based
processing.

Weaknesses: Slow (1-3 day settlement in many corridors), opaque FX pricing, high per-
transaction cost ($25-50 for cross-border wires), multiple intermediary hops. De-risking
(correspondent bank exits from high-risk corridors) is reducing coverage and increasing
concentration risk.

Strategic outlook: SWIFT itself is adapting — ISO 20022, pre-validation APIs, SWIFT Go for
smaller cross-border payments. But the structural pressure from Wise/Airwallex, stablecoin rails,
and Visa/Mastercard B2B Connect is real. The correspondent banking profit pool will compress
20-35% over 5 years as fintech and blockchain-based alternatives capture the most price-

sensitive corridors. Large-value institutional flows stay on SWIFT/Fedwire/CHIPS. SMB and
consumer remittance flows are the contested territory.

International / Cross-Border Payments

Current role: Cross-border payments market is worth over $194 trillion and is forecast to reach
$320 trillion by 2032. US is the largest source of remittance flows globally. Cross-border B2B
payments are the highest-fee, highest-friction segment — and the most disrupted.

Economics: Banks capture FX spread of 1.5-3% on retail cross-border payments, plus wire fees.
Fintechs like Wise operate at 0.3-0.7% all-in for major corridors. The spread compression is
massive — early Wise corridors saw 80%+ fee reductions vs. banks. For B2B: Airwallex, Nium,
Ebury, and Part quote interbank rates plus small FX margin for SMB flows.

Key players: JPMorgan Payments, Citi Treasury & Trade Solutions (large corporate). Wise,
Airwallex, Nium, Ebury, Currencycloud (acquired by Visa), Ripple, Remitly, Western Union
(declining). Airwallex reached $1 billion in annualized revenue in October 2025, processing more
than $130 billion in annualized TPV.

Strengths of challengers: Real exchange rates, transparent pricing, local payment network
access in 50+ markets, API-first, fast settlement.

Strategic outlook: The most disrupted segment in payments over the next 5 years. Stablecoins
are a potentially transformative force here — over 50% of companies surveyed expect to adopt
stablecoins for cross-border payments in the next 6-12 months, expecting stablecoins to handle
5-10% of cross-border payments by 2030, equivalent to $2.1-4.2 trillion according to EY
estimates. The key constraint is last-mile fiat conversion and regulatory compliance in
destination markets. Whoever solves programmatic FX conversion with stablecoin rails at scale
wins the next era of cross-border B2B payments.

Digital Wallets and Mobile Payments

Current role: Digital wallets accounted for almost 50% of online transactions in 2023, and their
usage is expected to surpass 54% by 2025. Apple Pay and Google Pay dominate contactless in-
store in the US. PayPal dominates online checkout. Venmo/Cash App own P2P. Shop Pay
(Shopify) is the fastest-growing checkout wallet in e-commerce.

Economics: Wallets are primarily credential storage layers on top of existing rails (mostly card,
some ACH/A2A). Apple earns ~15bps per credit card transaction routed through Apple Pay from
issuers. Google Pay monetizes less directly — data and ecosystem lock-in. PayPal earns 1.5-2%
take rate on transactions. Cash App monetizes through Bitcoin, instant transfer fees, and Cash
App Card interchange. Venmo monetizes via Pay with Venmo merchant fees.


=== SEITE 09 ===

Key players: Apple Pay (iPhone NFC dominance), Google Pay, PayPal, Venmo, Cash App,
Samsung Pay, Shop Pay, Amazon Pay, Walmart Pay.

Strategic outlook: Wallets are becoming the primary consumer payments interface — not the
rail. The strategic importance is in owning the checkout experience and consumer identity,
which confers data leverage and the ability to route across underlying rails. Apple's NFC
monopoly in iPhone (now being partially opened by EU regulation, with US implications likely)
has been a moat. The most important long-term wallet dynamic: whoever controls the checkout
flow controls routing decisions — this is why Visa and Mastercard are investing in tokenization
and network-level wallet integrations rather than fighting wallets. Stablecoin-linked wallets
(PayPal's PYUSD, Coinbase's Base network) represent a potential future where wallets settle on
non-card rails.

Stablecoins

Current role: The most strategically important emerging payment infrastructure in the US.
Outstanding USD stablecoins reached nearly $280 billion at year-end 2025. The GENIUS Act
(July 2025) created the first federal regulatory framework for payment stablecoins. The OCC
conditionally granted national trust bank charters to Circle, Paxos, and three other nonbank
financial firms in December 2025.

Economics: Issuer earns T-bill yield on reserve assets backing stablecoins. With $280B
outstanding and short-term T-bills yielding ~4.5%, this represents roughly $12-13B in annual
float income — a revenue pool that did not exist five years ago. Transaction fees on stablecoin
rails are near-zero (gas fees on USDC/Base transactions are fractions of a cent). The economic
model is: issue stablecoin at near-zero transaction cost, earn float on reserves, retain users in
ecosystem for other services.

Key players: Circle (USDC — dominant in institutional and B2B), Paxos (PYUSD, USDP), Tether
(USDT — offshore), Coinbase (USDC co-issuer, Base L2 network), Fireblocks (settlement rails
for institutional stablecoin flows), Ripple (RLUSD for ODL), bank issuers under GENIUS Act.

Strengths: Near-instant settlement (seconds on L2 networks). Global reach without
correspondent banking. Programmability (smart contracts for conditional payments, escrow,
streaming payments). Float to the issuer (not the merchant or consumer). GENIUS Act provides
regulatory clarity for the first time.

Weaknesses: No consumer protection equivalent to card chargebacks. Last-mile fiat conversion
friction. Regulatory complexity in non-US jurisdictions. GENIUS Act prohibits interest/yield to
holders — limiting consumer adoption incentive.

Strategic outlook: The most transformative 5-year bet in payments. Use cases accreting fastest:
(1) cross-border B2B (supplier payments, treasury corridors); (2) institutional settlement
(tokenized securities, repo); (3) gig economy worker payouts; (4) remittances. Non-obvious

insight: Banks issuing stablecoins under GENIUS Act can capture the float income that
currently accrues to Circle — this is why JPMorgan (JPM Coin for institutional), Citi, and BofA
are all moving aggressively into tokenized deposit and stablecoin issuance. The real question is
not whether stablecoins grow — they will — but whether the economic gains from reserve float

Other Crypto Payment Mechanisms

Current role: Minimal for actual commerce payments. Bitcoin is primarily a store of value and
speculative asset, not a payment instrument (high transaction costs on base layer, volatility).
Lightning Network (Bitcoin L2) enables micropayments with low fees but adoption is limited.
Ethereum smart contracts are the backbone of DeFi but not consumer payments. Ripple/XRP is
used in specific institutional cross-border corridors.

Strategic outlook: Non-stablecoin crypto for payments remains a niche use case except in
specific remittance corridors where recipients prefer crypto-native settlement (e.g., Latin
America, Sub-Saharan Africa). The focus of institutional energy has shifted entirely to
stablecoins and tokenized deposits. Bitcoin's role in payments may be permanently limited by
volatility and the availability of superior alternatives (stablecoins).

3. KEY PLAYERS AND COMPETITIVE DYNAMICS

Traditional Incumbents

JPMorgan Chase: The most powerful player in US payments by any measure. Largest card issuer
(~$1.4T in card sales volume), dominant correspondent bank, owns Paymentech (large merchant
acquirer), has deployed JPM Coin for institutional cross-border, and is investing aggressively in
tokenized deposits. JPM's competitive advantage is the combination of consumer deposits,
credit extension, merchant relationships, and institutional infrastructure — no competitor has all
four. Greatest risk: if embedded finance platforms disintermediate the merchant relationship,
JPM loses cross-sell leverage.

Visa / Mastercard: The most structurally resilient players in the US market. Moats: (1) network
ubiquity — every merchant, every bank; (2) token vaults — 10B+ network tokens issued, making
tokenized card credentials the default secure credential; (3) data — the only players with full
visibility across issuer and acquirer side of transactions; (4) value-added services (fraud tools,
analytics, Visa Direct / Mastercard Send for push payments) now represent ~35% of network
revenue and growing. Key risk: CCCA (Credit Card Competition Act) routing mandates for credit
cards, which would compress network fee economics by forcing network competition. As part of
the Visa/Mastercard interchange settlement in 2024, Visa agreed to hold off on interchange
increases for five years.


=== SEITE 11 ===

Fiserv / FIS / Global Payments: The processing oligopoly. Between them, they handle the
majority of US merchant acquiring volume. Revenue is increasingly SaaS-oriented (Clover,
WorldPay). Margin compression from Adyen, Stripe is a persistent threat at the upper end of the
market. Their defense: scale, SaaS bundles (Clover = POS + software + payments), and
SMB/community bank relationships that fintech competitors cannot easily replicate.

American Express: The most profitable card issuer/network by net income per card. Closed-loop
model gives Amex unique data advantages and eliminates interchange passthrough. Amex's key
strategic move: expansion into SMB and B2B cards, where interchange is higher and the rewards
subsidy is more manageable. Risk: premium consumer reward cost inflation and merchant
surcharge acceptance.

Fintech Challengers

Stripe: The most important private payments infrastructure company. Estimated $1T+ in annual
TPV, $15B+ in revenue. Stripe's edge: (1) developer-first API experience that has become the
default for SaaS and e-commerce; (2) Stripe Radar (AI fraud — cuts card testing attacks by 80%
per company data); (3) Stripe Connect (marketplace/platform payments); (4) expanding into
financial services (Stripe Capital, Stripe Treasury, Stripe Issuing). Risk: Adyen is winning the
enterprise mid-market; Stripe's move upmarket is contested.

Adyen: The enterprise PSP challenger. Outperforming legacy processors on gross margin due to:
direct acquiring (no third-party acquirer margin), single platform architecture, and lower total
cost of ownership for large merchants. Adyen's "unified commerce" thesis — single platform for
in-store and online — is winning enterprise retailers. Key limitation: less suited for SMB or long-
tail merchants than Stripe.

Block/Square: Dominant in SMB POS and Cash App ecosystem. Cash App is a financial services
super-app with 57M+ monthly actives. Square's Afterpay acquisition brought BNPL to the suite.
The risk: Cash App's monetization is heavily dependent on Bitcoin trading spread and Instant
Transfer fees — both under competitive pressure.

PayPal: The dominant online checkout wallet (3.5B+ PayPal buttons on the web). Revenue under
pressure as Venmo monetization matures, PayPal faces competition from Shop Pay/Apple Pay in
checkout, and the company navigates a strategic transition toward AI-driven personalized
checkout. PYUSD (PayPal stablecoin) is a meaningful stablecoin bet — volume growing but
mostly ecosystem-internal.

Plaid: The infrastructure layer for open banking in the US. Connects consumer bank accounts to
~8,000 fintech apps via API. Revenue: per-API-call fees for account verification, instant ACH,
identity. Strategic position: the data broker/aggregator sitting between banks and fintechs. Risk:
bank direct access APIs (Chase, Citi building their own connectivity APIs), and CFPB 1033

rulemaking mandating open banking data portability — which both enables and commoditizes
Plaid's core service.

Modern Treasury: B2B payment operations infrastructure. Connects to ACH, Fedwire, RTP,
FedNow via bank APIs with workflow automation for reconciliation, money movement, and
treasury operations. Represents the "payments operating system" thesis — software sitting

above the rails, not on them.

Margeta: Card issuing infrastructure. Powers Cash App, Uber, DoorDash, Klarna, and hundreds
of BaaS fintechs. Revenue: interchange split with issuing banks and per-transaction fees. Risk:
Stripe Issuing, Lithic, Highnote as competitors; margin pressure as issuers negotiate harder.

Cross-Border Specialists

Wise: The gold standard for transparent, low-cost international transfers. $100B+ in annual
cross-border volume. Competitive advantage: mid-market FX rate transparency, proprietary
direct bank integrations in 80+ markets (bypassing correspondent banks), and network effects
in payment flow matching. Risk: Airwallex and neobanks replicating the value proposition with
broader feature sets.

Airwallex: The fastest-growing cross-border payment infrastructure company. Airwallex
reached $1 billion in ARR in October 2025, now repositioning as an embedded finance platform
— 60% of revenue comes from its API and products. Strategic shift from FX spread to embedded
finance SaaS is raising take rates and reducing commoditization risk.

Nium: B2B payment infrastructure in 100+ countries. Particularly strong in Southeast Asia and
embedded finance distribution. SWIFT alternative for specific corridors.

Ripple / ODL: Uses XRP as a bridge currency for cross-border settlement in specific corridors
(primarily USD/MXN, USD/PHP). Niche but growing in markets where traditional
correspondent banking is expensive or unreliable.

Wallet / Platform Ecosystems

The Key insight: wallets are becoming the consumer identity layer in payments, not just a
credential holder. Apple Pay, Google Pay, and PayPal each have 100M+ US users with stored
payment credentials. The strategic importance: whoever controls the checkout UI controls (a)
routing decisions, (b) consumer data, and (c) the upsell into BNPL, rewards, and financial
services.

Shop Pay (Shopify): The fastest-growing checkout button in US e-commerce. Shopify's ~25% of
US e-commerce GMV gives Shop Pay significant network effects. Risk: Shopify's merchant
concentration and fee structure make it a competitor to payment processors, not just a wallet.

Amazon Pay: Primarily used for off-Amazon sites. Limited network effect outside Amazon
ecosystem.

Crypto / Stablecoin Infrastructure Players

Circle: Dominant USDC issuer. The primary beneficiary of GENIUS Act regulatory clarity.
Circle's moat: USDC is the most trusted, regulated, and institutionally-accepted USD stablecoin.
Circle's challenge: it must build distribution and use case adoption faster than banks (who now
can issue their own stablecoins) replicate the trust and compliance infrastructure.

Fireblocks: Institutional-grade digital asset custody and settlement infrastructure. The

4, TECHNOLOGY AND INFRASTRUCTURE SHIFTS

Real-Time Rails and 24/7 Settlement

The transition from batch (ACH, checks) to real-time (RTP, FedNow) is not a 5-year project — it is
structurally underway. The challenge: real-time is irrevocable, which means fraud prevention
must be pre-transaction (AI-based risk scoring) not post-transaction (dispute/chargeback
resolution). This changes the value proposition entirely for fraud vendors — from dispute
management to pre-authorization AI.

API-First and Cloud-Native Infrastructure

The shift from on-premise core banking to cloud-native API infrastructure (Galileo, Margeta,
Unit, Synctera, Temenos SaaS) is enabling fintechs to launch in months rather than years. This
has dramatically lowered barriers to entry at the fintech layer but increased concentration risk at
the infrastructure provider layer. Galileo (now SoFi) and Margeta each power hundreds of
fintechs — a Galileo outage disrupts the entire ecosystem.

Payment Orchestration / Smart Routing

Orchestration is the fastest-growing software layer in merchant payments. Merchants with
>$50M in payment volume are increasingly deploying multi-PSP strategies with smart routing
based on: authorization rate by BIN/card type/geography, transaction cost, fraud score, latency.
Providers: Spreedly (150+ payment connectors), Primer (no-code orchestration), Gravy (cloud
routing), Checkout.com (orchestration + acquiring), Adyen (unified commerce). Key strategic
insight: as orchestration commoditizes the acquiring layer, the winners will be the orchestration
platforms with the richest decision intelligence (AI-based routing optimization), not the ones
with the most connectors.

Tokenization and Network-Linked Wallets

EMV Co (Visa/Mastercard/Amex/Discover consortium) network tokens have replaced primary
account numbers (PANs) in mobile wallets and many e-commerce flows. This has a profound
strategic implication: tokens are managed by the network (Visa Token Service, Mastercard
Digital Enablement Service), which means the networks control lifecycle management of
payment credentials even as the consumer-facing experience moves to Apple Pay or Google Pay.
Networks become more embedded, not less, in the digital wallet era.

Embedded Finance and Software-Led Distribution

The "embedded payments" thesis: payment functionality built directly into vertical SaaS
(Shopify Payments, Toast POS, ServiceTitan field service, FareHarbor activities booking).
Merchant retention for embedded payments providers is structurally higher than for standalone
processors because switching requires replacing the entire software stack. Take rates are also
structurally higher — merchants pay for convenience and integration, not just transaction


=== SEITE 15 ===

processing. This is why Stripe, PayFac-in-a-box providers (Payrix, Finix, Infinicept), and Adyen
for Platforms are growing faster than traditional acquirers.

Open Banking / Account Connectivity

CFPB's 1033 final rule (effective 2024-2026 by institution size) mandates financial data
portability — consumers can direct banks to share their financial data with third parties via API.
This accelerates Plaid, MX Technologies, Akoya, and Finicity (acquired by Mastercard). The
strategic implications: (1) reduces Plaid's screen-scraping friction (which creates
compliance/security issues); (2) enables richer A2A payment initiation; (3) shifts bank data from
captive asset to regulated utility. Non-obvious second-order effect: open banking data
connectivity + Al analytics = real-time underwriting of creditworthiness without credit bureau
dependency. This threatens FICO and traditional credit decisioning.

Stablecoins and Blockchain-Based Settlement

The GENIUS Act framework has legitimized stablecoin infrastructure. The Key architectural
shift: programmable settlement (smart contracts can automate conditional payments, escrow
release, streaming payroll, and DvP for securities) is not possible on ACH or Fedwire. This creates
entirely new payment product categories — not just cheaper versions of existing ones.

5. AIDISRUPTION LAYER

Where AI Changes Economics vs. Where It Only Improves Efficiency

This distinction is critical for strategy. AI changes economics when it: (a) enables new revenue
streams that didn't previously exist; (b) reduces unit costs to near-zero in ways that change
pricing power; (c) creates data moats that compound over time. Al improves efficiency when it:
(a) automates human-labor-intensive tasks without changing the underlying fee structure; (b)
reduces fraud losses; (c) speeds up workflows.

Real-Time Fraud Scoring: Economics Changed

Pre-Al fraud detection was rules-based (velocity checks, blacklists, hard thresholds). AI models
trained on billions of transactions reduce false positive rates by 30-50% (more revenue for
merchants), reduce false negative rates (fewer fraud losses for issuers), and enable dynamic risk
scoring that adapts to new fraud patterns in near-real-time. Stripe's Al-driven Radar tool cut card
testing attacks by an impressive 80%, analyzing billions of data points in real time. The economic
impact is material: lower fraud losses = lower interchange rates required to subsidize losses =
structural pressure on current interchange levels. AI fraud tools are also creating a new revenue
pool: fraud-as-a-service, where networks, fintechs (Sift, Sardine), and processors charge per-
transaction risk scoring fees to issuers and merchants.


=== SEITE 16 ===

AML / KYC / KYB Automation: Efficiency Improvement Now, Economics Shift Later

Legacy AML systems generate ~95% false positive rates on transaction monitoring alerts. AI
reduces this to ~70-80%, dramatically cutting the cost of compliance investigations. For large
banks spending $500M-$2B annually on BSA/AML compliance, this is massive efficiency gain.
The economic shift: AI-native compliance vendors (ComplyAdvantage, Hawk AI, Unit21) are
disrupting the legacy compliance software market (Oracle FCRM, Actimize/NICE, Temenos
Financial Crime Mitigation). Compliance SaaS unit economics improve as AI models scale — a
fundamental shift toward recurring SaaS revenue replacing high-labor compliance operations.

Non-obvious insight: as AI automates AML decision-making, banks face a new regulatory risk
— model explainability. Regulators (FinCEN, OCC, FRB) require explainable AI for SAR filing
and examination purposes. This creates demand for explainable-AI (XAI) tooling in compliance
— anew sub-category of compliance software.

Payment Operations Automation: Efficiency Now, Economics Shift Possible

Reconciliation, exceptions handling, return processing, and dispute investigations are among the
most labor-intensive payment operations. AI agents (Generative AI + workflow automation) can
automate 60-80% of routine exception handling. Modern Treasury, Stripe, and Adyen are
already deploying LLM-based payment operations tools internally. The economics shift: if
payment operations automation reduces total cost of ownership for merchant payment
infrastructure, net margins for PSPs could expand even as transaction fees compress —
decoupling revenue from labor cost.

Smart Routing Optimization

AlI-based payment routing (not just rule-based routing) optimizes authorization rates, fraud
scores, cost, and latency simultaneously across acquirers and payment methods. Providers with
richer cross-merchant data can build superior routing models — this creates a data network
effect where transaction volume generates model improvement that improves authorization
rates, which attracts more volume. Primer, Spreedly, and Adyen (with its unified platform data)
are building toward this. The key insight: this is where AI changes economics — better
authorization rates directly translate to higher revenue capture for merchants, creating
willingness to pay for intelligence-layer products on top of commodity processing.

Customer Support and Merchant Analytics: Efficiency Improvement

LLM-based customer service (Stripe Sigma, Adyen Analytics, PayPal's AI checkout assistant)
reduces support costs and improves merchant experience. This is efficiency, not an economic
model shift. The exception: Al-powered merchant analytics that generate actionable revenue
insights (e.g., "your authorization rate in Germany drops 15% on Amex — here's what to do")
have upsell value and can support premium SaaS tiers.


=== SEITE 17 ===

Transition to Autonomous Decisioning

The medium-term trajectory: fully autonomous AI agents for payment operations. This means
Al systems that (a) identify exceptions, (b) investigate root causes by querying data sources, (c)
remediate issues (re-route, re-submit, flag for human review) without human intervention. Early
implementations are live at Stripe, Adyen, and large bank treasury operations. This trajectory
creates existential risk for payment operations BPO firms (Conduent, Genpact payment
processing) and cost reduction opportunity for any firm currently paying for manual exception
handling.

6. COMPETITIVE DYNAMICS AND SHIFTING CONTROL POINTS

How Control Is Shifting Across the Stack

The traditional control point was the issuing bank — it owned the consumer relationship, the
credit product, and the payment credential. This control point is fragmenting. Wallets (Apple
Pay) now own the consumer checkout experience. Platforms (Shopify, Toast) own the merchant
relationship. Orchestration layers own the routing logic. AI vendors own the risk decisioning.
The bank retains the deposit relationship and credit extension — which remain powerful — but
the intermediary economics at each layer are being competed down.

The key shift: control is moving toward players with superior data and intelligence capabilities.
Visa and Mastercard retain control because they see the full transaction (both sides) and can
monetize that data through value-added services. Stripe retains control because its fraud and
analytics models are trained on aggregate multi-merchant data. The players losing control:
commodity acquirers and processors who have transaction volume but not the intelligence layer
to monetize it.

Cards vs. A2A: The Real Dynamic

Cards will not be displaced in consumer discretionary payments in the next 5 years. The reasons:
(1) rewards economics create consumer lock-in; (2) zero-liability protection has no real-time
payment equivalent; (3) credit functionality (revolving balance, installments) is native to cards,
not A2A. Where displacement is real and accelerating: (a) bill pay and recurring billers who will
shift to A2A/FedNow to capture the lower cost and improve float; (b) B2B AP where commercial
card interchange is the most expensive rail; (c) government disbursements (IRS refunds,
insurance claims, earned wage access). The consumer debit card is more vulnerable than credit
— it has no rewards moat and higher regulatory interchange caps.

Checks and SWIFT: Decline Trajectories

Checks: structural decline, not disruption. The volume trajectory is a 6-8% annual decline that
will continue. The remaining volume (mostly B2B) will transition to ACH Same-Day and RTP


=== SEITE 18 ===

over the next 5-7 years. There is no scenario where check volume stabilizes.

SWIFT: structural redefinition, not simple decline. ISO 20022 migration is modernizing SWIFT.
SWIFT Go is addressing sub-$10K international transfers. SWIFT itself will survive because it
solves the interoperability problem no single bilateral network can. But SWIFT's role as the only
practical cross-border messaging standard is ending as stablecoin corridors and bilateral API
connections emerge. SWIFT becomes one option among several for cross-border.

Embedded Payments and Customer Ownership

Embedded payments fundamentally change pricing power because switching cost is the entire
software stack, not just the payment processor. Toast's take rate (~2.49% + flat fee) is 30-SObps
higher than a merchant could achieve by buying standalone payment processing — and
merchants accept it because the integration value is worth it. This is the embedded finance
premium. The implication: payment revenue is becoming SaaS revenue, and the players with
vertical SaaS distribution (Shopify, Toast, ServiceTitan, Mindbody) will expand payment take
rates even as standalone processor margins compress.

Commoditization vs. Strategic Premium

Commoditizing: Plain-vanilla acquiring (sub-SObps margin), generic gateway functionality,
screen-scraping open banking, basic ACH initiation, rules-based fraud tools, legacy core
banking.

Gaining strategic premium: AI-based fraud and risk scoring (data network effects), stablecoin
issuance/reserve management, payment orchestration with routing intelligence, embedded
payments in vertical SaaS, cross-border infrastructure with proprietary local network access,
tokenization infrastructure (controlled by networks), and compliance automation with
explainable AI for regulated entities.

Risk level: Medium over 3 years, high over 5 years. The CCCA has been introduced multiple
times but not passed — however, the political pressure from merchants is persistent. If CCCA
passes, the impact on Visa/Mastercard network fee revenue and issuer interchange could be

8. REGULATORY AND POLICY LANDSCAPE

Interchange Regulation

The Durbin Amendment (2010) capped debit interchange for large issuers at ~$0.21 + 0.05%.
The Federal Reserve proposed expanding Durbin coverage (lower cap) in 2023 — the rule would
cut regulated debit interchange by ~30%, affecting JPMorgan, BofA, Wells Fargo's debit issuer
economics. This has not been finalized as of March 2026 amid legal challenges.

The Credit Card Competition Act (credit card routing mandate) has repeatedly been introduced
but not passed. If enacted, it would be the most significant structural change to card economics
since Durbin.

CFPB and Consumer Protection

CFPB 1033 open banking rules are in implementation phase, mandating data portability for
consumer financial accounts. This is the regulatory catalyst for US open banking — accelerating
Plaid, Akoya, Finicity, and bank-direct API access. CFPB has also targeted BNPL (Regulation Z
extension for periodic statement and dispute rights) — adding compliance cost to BNPL
providers but legitimizing the product category for mainstream adoption.

Stablecoin Regulation (GENIUS Act, July 2025)

The most important payments regulatory development of 2025. The GENIUS Act establishes a
regulatory framework for payment stablecoins, prohibiting any person other than a permitted
payment stablecoin issuer from issuing a payment stablecoin in the US. Key provisions: 100%


=== SEITE 22 ===

reserve requirement (US dollars and short-term Treasuries), AML/BSA obligations for stablecoin
issuers, no interest/yield to holders, state-level regime available for issuers under $10B
outstanding.

Strategic implications: (1) Tether (USDT) cannot legally serve US users without compliance;
this could redistribute substantial market share to Circle (USDC) and bank-issued stablecoins.
(2) Big Tech companies (Apple, Google, Amazon) cannot issue stablecoins without a GENIUS
Act license unless they spin off a compliant subsidiary — the "Stablecoin Certification Review
Committee" creates a regulatory hurdle for non-financial Big Tech. (3) Banks that issue
stablecoins capture reserve float that currently accrues to Circle — a direct competitive threat to
Circle's business model.

AML/BSA Expectations

FinCEN is modernizing BSA rules (AML/CFT Program Effectiveness final rule, 2024) with a risk-
based approach that requires actual effectiveness measurement, not just checkbox compliance.
This creates demand for AI-based compliance tools that can demonstrate measurable reduction
in financial crime risk.

Crypto Regulatory Clarity

The Federal Reserve rescinded its anti-crypto guidance in late 2025 and replaced it with a
"different activity, different risks" framework. The OCC granted national trust bank charters to
Circle, Paxos, and other nonbank stablecoin issuers in December 2025. This opens the door to
federally chartered nonbank stablecoin issuers operating at national scale without state-by-state
licensing.

Real-Time Payment Access

The Federal Reserve is considering offering limited "skinny" master accounts to federally
regulated stablecoin issuers — giving them access to FedNow and Fedwire without full bank
charter requirements. This would be a historic expansion of access to the Federal Reserve
payment system to non-bank entities.

(2) Expand into stablecoin rails for cross-border. (3) Build out financial services beyond
payments (lending, treasury, insurance) to increase revenue per customer and switching cost.

Cross-Border Specialists (Wise, Airwallex, Nium): (1) Expand local payment network coverage
— this is the hardest-to-replicate moat. (2) Build stablecoin-to-fiat conversion capabilities to
serve the growing USDC/stablecoin payment corridor. (3) Move from pure-play FX/payments to
full treasury infrastructure for SMB.

Al/Compliance Fintechs (Sardine, Alloy, Socure, Comply Advantage): (1) Build out model
explainability for regulatory purposes — this is the gate to enterprise bank sales. (2) Expand
beyond KYC/fraud to full transaction monitoring and AML workflow automation. (3)
Accumulate cross-industry training data to widen the moat against bank in-house AI buildout.
